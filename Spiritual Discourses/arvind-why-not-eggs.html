Why Not Eggs?
Understanding the Living Being Behind the Shell

Many thoughtful people say: “Market eggs are unfertilized. There is no chick inside. So what is the harm?” The question is fair. The answer becomes clear when we look at the living bird who produces the egg — not at the egg alone.

1. The argument is not about the embryo

The usual debate focuses on whether a fertilized egg contains a sentient being. Commercial eggs sold in shops are almost always unfertilized. No embryo develops. That part is correct.

Yet the real point is different. Eggs (like milk and honey) come from already living, sentient creatures. To obtain them we must use and manage the bodies of those creatures. The question is not “Is there a chick inside?” but “What does the living hen experience so that this egg can reach my plate?”

2. What an egg actually is

An egg is the ovulation of a hen. In biological terms it is a form of reproductive discharge — comparable in its natural cycle to a menstrual process. The white shell and the contents are built from nutrients drawn from the hen’s own body: calcium from her bones, proteins and fats from her bloodstream and tissues.

When we eat an egg we are consuming material that the bird’s body assembled for its own reproductive cycle. The shell is not empty of connection; it is the outer casing of a process that happened inside a living being.

3. Natural cycle versus industrial demand

In nature a hen lays roughly 12 to 15 eggs in a year — enough for the continuation of her species when conditions allow.

Through selective breeding and genetic manipulation over generations, commercial hens have been changed so that they now lay almost one egg every single day. Their bodies are pushed far beyond the original design of the species.

This is not a natural “gift” the bird freely offers. It is a continuous, high-volume production demanded of a body that was never meant to work at that rate.

4. The cost paid by the living bird

The constant daily laying draws heavily on the hen’s physical resources. Calcium is pulled from her skeleton; energy and nutrients are diverted again and again to form each new egg. Many birds develop bone weakness, reproductive disorders, and chronic exhaustion.

The suffering is not abstract. It is the lived experience of a female body forced into continuous reproductive output. A human woman who was made to experience a comparable monthly cycle every single day of the year would recognize the strain. The physiology is different, yet the principle of over-use of a living reproductive system is the same.

5. When production falls

After one or two years of intense laying, most commercial hens can no longer produce eggs at the required rate. Their bodies are depleted.

At that point they are no longer considered profitable for egg production. They are sold to slaughterhouses and killed for their flesh. The industry extracts the last economic value from the same body that has already been used for eggs.

This is the ordinary, planned end of life in the conventional egg industry — not an exception, but the standard cycle.

6. The same pattern in milk and honey

Eggs are not unique. Milk and honey follow the same logic:

  • Milk requires the continuous reproductive and lactating use of a living mother cow or buffalo.
  • Honey is taken from the organised labour and stored food of living bee colonies.

In each case the product comes from a sentient being whose body or life-work is directed toward human use. The ethical question is therefore larger than any single item: it is about whether we are willing to treat living beings as production units.

7. A simple invitation

No one needs to be shamed. Awareness itself is the beginning of change.

When we see clearly that an egg is not a free-floating object but the product of a living hen’s body under industrial pressure, the choice becomes simpler. Many people who once ate eggs daily later stop — not because of dogma, but because the understanding settled in the heart.

Compassion does not require perfection overnight. It only asks that we look honestly at the living being whose life makes the product possible, and then decide what we can live with in peace.

The egg on the plate looks quiet and ordinary. Behind it stands a living bird whose body was used, day after day, beyond its natural rhythm, and who is finally discarded when she can no longer produce.

Seeing this does not make us superior. It only makes us more awake. From that wakefulness a gentler choice can arise — for the birds, and for the quality of our own conscience.

— Arvind Animal Activist
