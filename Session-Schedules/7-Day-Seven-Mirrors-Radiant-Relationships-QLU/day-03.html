Seven Mirrors of Radiant Relationships · Day 3

Mirror 3 · The Mirror of Loss (Childhood)

What Was Lost, Given Away or Taken Away

The third mirror reflects something we lost, gave away, or had taken away — usually in childhood. You might have lost your innocence, your confidence, your courage, your creativity, or your smile. As a child you were full of laughter; as an adult you became serious.

This mirror reveals the mystery of many romantic relationships.

The Party Attraction – Smile and Confidence

At a party a young woman with a mysterious smile opens your heart. You want to talk to her. That smile enchanted you. It has nothing to do with her, actually — it is mirroring your loss. You missed your own smile when you were a child and became a very serious person. The smile of the woman has made you feel: “Yes, this is the quality I have missed.”

The same woman falls in love with you because you look confident. She lost her confidence in childhood after a deep blow to her self-esteem (“I am not good enough”). When she met you, confident and speaking easily with people, she felt the attraction instantly.

Both feel: “We are soulmates, inseparable, Romeo and Juliet.” For a while it is “I like ice cream — I also like ice cream; I like movies — I also like movies.” After one year the honeymoon ends. Now: “I like ice cream — I hate ice cream; I like movies — I hate movies.” They realise the attraction was to the quality each was missing. The other person only reminded them of what they had lost. The other cannot complete them. The dream never gets fulfilled and they fall out of love.

You Are Already Complete

Other persons can never substitute or complete you — though love stories say “without you I am incomplete.” You are already complete; you have not yet recognised it. Every quality you find enchanting in others is already present in you, though you have not opened it in your consciousness. As children we disowned courage, intelligence, innocence, spontaneity, creativity. We find those qualities interesting in people because we have not yet recognised them in ourselves.

What inspires you in your heroes and heroines is what you have to find in your own self. Meditation is the way to find it. All these beautiful qualities are already there within us.

Beggar Relationships

Most relationships of this type are like beggar relationships. A beggar has a lack, an emptiness. Husband and wife come together and each is asking the other: “Give me love, give me love.” How can another beggar give you what he does not have? These two beggars will give birth to more beggars — the same lack is perpetuated.

We feel romantic and idealistic and treat the other person as the solution. That is never the way. These are complementary relationships: we fall in love and we fall out of love. After the honeymoon we fight again, because we have not yet awakened the disowned aspect in our own consciousness.

Your consciousness is the fundamental. It has everything. It is the God; it is all-in-all. There is nothing that we are lacking. Relationships remind us of this powerful insight: what we are looking for in other people we have to find in our own self.

Love then becomes sharing rather than making the other person responsible for your joy. “My wife must make me happy” — how can she? Your joy is in you. She can trigger and share it; through sharing we multiply the joy. What you have not already found in your own consciousness, no other person in the world can give you.

Conditional Parenting and the Empty Hole

If parenting is conditional — the child never experiences enough hugs, touch, care and time — the child feels rejected, abandoned, lonely. Later the adult tries to fill that empty hole through relationships and ends up in addictive, co-dependent or parasitic relationships.

Jane Fonda said: do not get married until you know who you are and what you are looking for. When we do not know who we are and still enter relationship, we make a big mess.

Khalil Gibran in The Prophet said: “Let there be spaces in your togetherness.” Even if you married without fully realising yourself, give space for each other to grow, to understand, to go deeper into the wisdom.

Difficult People as Catalysts

Relationships can be catalysts for spiritual growth and greater ascension. We do not need to leave wives and husbands and go to the Himalayas. We can stay in samsara — with nagging wives or harsh husbands — and learn. All relationships can become indirect teachers if we open this dimension in consciousness.

Stop judgment, stop blaming, stop criticism. Look into yourself: Why did I draw this person into my life? What choice did I make? What lesson is this person teaching me? What did I miss or lose in myself that I am trying to find in this person?

Socrates and Xanthippe

Socrates used to praise his wife Xanthippe — a famously difficult, nagging wife. She would pour boiling water on his head; he had bumps from being hit. He said that because of her he became a great philosopher. She triggered his spiritual quest; his tolerance increased; he became impeccable.

The so-called difficult people can serve a wonderful purpose. They can make you progress more than years of solitary meditation. You may meditate for a decade, yet the person living with you can teach you more in a short time. It is a blessing in disguise. Do not consider difficult relationships only as disaster. Look from a different viewpoint: Why did I attract this person? What choice did I make? Unless you asked for it at some level, it would not come into your experience. It is choice-based — sometimes unconscious, but still a choice.

Key Remember Points

  • The third mirror reflects childhood loss — smile, confidence, innocence, creativity, courage, trust.
  • We are drawn to the quality we ourselves have disowned; the other only reminds us.
  • You are already complete; the other cannot complete you.
  • “Beggar relationships” ask for what neither person fully has and perpetuate the lack.
  • Love becomes sharing, not demand that the other supply your missing joy.
  • Difficult people can be powerful catalysts — sometimes faster than long meditation.
  • Ask: What did I lose? What am I trying to find in this person that I must reclaim in myself?

Homework for Day 3

  • Sit for ten minutes. Ask: “As a child, what did I lose, give away, or have taken from me?” Write freely (smile, trust, playfulness, feeling of being enough…).
  • Look at one important relationship. What quality first attracted you? Does it point to something you need to recover in yourself?
  • Practise one small act of reclaiming that quality today.
  • If a relationship is difficult, write: “What lesson might this person be teaching me? What choice drew them into my life?”
