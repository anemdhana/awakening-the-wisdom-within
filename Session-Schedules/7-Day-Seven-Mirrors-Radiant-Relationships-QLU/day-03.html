Seven Mirrors of Radiant Relationships · Day 3

Mirror 3 · The Mirror of Loss (Childhood)

What This Mirror Reflects

The third mirror is the mirror of loss — especially what we lost, gave away, or had taken away in childhood. Every child has needs. In the process of trying to get those needs met, the child sometimes loses something precious: confidence, innocence, playfulness, smile, creativity, trust, or the feeling of being loved for who they are.

This mirror reveals why so many romantic and close relationships start with strong attraction and later turn into struggle.

Story – The Girl Who Lost Confidence & the Boy Who Lost Innocence

A six-year-old girl was happily dancing and singing at home. Her tired mother came back from work, saw the mess and the loud music, and shouted: “Stop this nonsense! You have an ugly voice. Go to your room.” The mother later rested and forgot. For the child, the mother’s words were absolute truth. She stopped singing and dancing. She lost her confidence.

A boy grew up with parents who fought daily and sometimes hit him. To survive that environment he had to become an “adult” early. He lost his innocence and playfulness.

Years later they meet at a party. He is dynamic and confident; she sits quietly, looking calm and “innocent.” Energetically they feel: “I have found my soulmate.” For a year everything is beautiful — “I like ice cream / I also like ice cream.” After the honeymoon phase they begin to fight. Each was only reflecting to the other what the other had lost. They had hoped the other would complete them. Two beggars cannot fill each other’s emptiness.

Beggar Relationships vs Completeness

Most romantic attractions of this type are complementary relationships. We are drawn to the quality we have disowned in ourselves. We feel the other person will complete us. That is not possible. You are already complete; you have only forgotten or disowned parts of yourself.

Dr Newton says: relationships of this kind are often like two beggars asking each other for money. A beggar cannot give what he does not have. We keep pleading “give me love, care for me” while the other is also empty in that place.

The enchanting quality you see in the other is already present in you — you have not yet opened it in your own consciousness. Meditation and inner work are the way to find it again.

What to Do with the Third Mirror

When someone strongly attracts or later frustrates you:

  • Ask: “What quality in this person enchanted me? What does that quality remind me I have lost or given away?”
  • Do not demand that the other person supply what you lost. Search where you lost it — usually in childhood — and reclaim it inside yourself.
  • If you lost something on the road, you will not find it by searching inside the house. Go to the place of loss (inner child work, memory, feeling) and recover it there.

Difficult relationships can become powerful teachers. Socrates praised his difficult wife because she increased his tolerance and spiritual quest. The so-called difficult person can teach you more in a short time than years of solitary meditation — if you are open.

Key Remember Points

  • The third mirror reflects childhood loss — confidence, innocence, smile, creativity, trust, unconditional love.
  • We often fall in love with the quality we ourselves have disowned.
  • The other person cannot complete you; they only remind you of what you need to reclaim.
  • “Beggar relationships” keep asking for what neither person fully has.
  • Find the lost quality inside yourself; then relationship becomes sharing, not demand.
  • Difficult people can be catalysts for rapid growth when seen as mirrors.

Homework for Day 3

  • Sit quietly for ten minutes. Ask: “As a child, what did I lose, give away, or have taken from me?” Write whatever comes (confidence, playfulness, voice, trust, feeling of being enough…).
  • Look at one important relationship. What quality first attracted you? Does it point to something you yourself need to recover?
  • Practise one small act of reclaiming: if you lost playfulness, do something playful without purpose; if you lost voice, speak one true sentence you usually swallow.
  • Remember: the other person is a reminder, not the supplier of what you lost.
