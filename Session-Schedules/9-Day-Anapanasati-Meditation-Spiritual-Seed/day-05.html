9-Day Anapanasati Meditation • Day 5

Karma Siddhanta · How to Get Freedom from Karma

Gratitude & Today’s Focus

Priyanka began by offering deep gratitude to her Guru, Brahmarshi Pitamaha Patriji, and to her guiding Guru Brahmarshi Premnath Sir.

Today’s topic is one of the most important: Karma Siddhanta — not only what karma is, but especially how we can become free from it.

We Have a Negative Feeling Towards the Word “Karma”

The moment we hear the word “karma”, most of us feel something negative — punishment, suffering, difficulty. Priyanka asked participants to type “I” if they also feel this way. Almost everyone does.

Why? Because we have never properly understood the meaning.

In simple language, karma means action / work. It can be good or it can be not-good. There is nothing inherently “bad” about the word itself.

The Universal Rule That Never Changes

In our homes we make rules — meal times, sleep times, etc. Those rules can change. But the rules of the Universe never change. They apply to us, to previous generations, and to future generations equally:

What you give is what you get.
Do good → good returns.
Do what is not right → that returns.

We have all experienced this. We have all heard it. But we have rarely understood it deeply and lived it.

“I Am a Good Person… Why Do I Still Suffer?”

A very common question arises: “I am a very good person. I have never troubled anyone. Yet my life is full of difficulties.”

This is where Spiritual Science becomes essential. We have taken many births. In those births we have done both good and not-so-good actions. The difficulties of this life are often the ripening of unfinished accounts from earlier lives. The soul brings only as much as it can currently bear — just as a 3rd-standard child is given 3rd-standard syllabus, not PhD subjects.

Karma is not instant in this birth because the soul would not be able to handle the full load at once.

Karma Is Not Punishment — It Is Education (Shikshana)

We usually translate karma as “punishment” (shikshe). Patriji and the tradition say it is actually shikshana — education / the process of correction.

Torkom Saraydarian’s thorn example is used:

  • A thorn enters the hand. As long as it stays, there is pain and trouble.
  • When we decide to remove it, there is temporary extra pain.
  • After it is removed, healing begins.
  • If we leave it because “removal hurts”, the wound can become infected and the situation can become far worse.

Our past not-right actions are like thorns already stuck in us. The difficulties of this life are the process of removing those thorns. It may hurt while they come out, but that is the only way to heal.

Two Boxes We Carry

Priyanka explained with a very simple picture:

We carry two boxes with us across lives:

  1. Good-karma box — every time we help someone without expectation, give wisdom, do dāna-dharma, the action goes into this box.
  2. Not-right-karma box — every time we hurt someone, cause pain, or harm any being (including animals and birds), the action goes into this box.

Whichever box fills first “falls on our head”. When the good box is full, blessings return as a good family, good children, sudden “luck” (which is not luck at all — it is the fruit of our own earlier good seeds). When the other box fills, difficulties return.

The Seed and the Fruit

First rule of karma: the kind of seed you sow is the kind of fruit you get.

If you plant a tomato seed, you cannot sit in front of the plant and demand mangoes. It is simply not possible. The same law operates in life. We cannot sow one kind of action and expect a completely different result.

Practical Q&A – Vegetarianism in a Non-Vegetarian Home

A participant asked: “Everyone in my house is non-vegetarian. How can I alone follow? If they pressure me, can I still cook for them?”

Priyanka’s clear guidance:

  • Seven people share the karma of non-vegetarian food: the one who kills, the one who sells, the one who buys, the one who cooks, the one who serves, the one who eats…
  • First decide for yourself: “I will be 100% vegetarian.” Do not wait for family approval. “What will they say?” is only a reason we give ourselves.
  • If you must cook for others, while cooking release the thought: “I truly do not like doing this. May compassion arise in everyone who will eat this. May they all become vegetarian. May they all become meditators.”
  • When your own energy and intention are clear, change begins to happen slowly in those who consume the food as well.

Another participant shared that everything in life is going well, yet there is no mental peace. Priyanka linked it directly: “As is the food, so is the mind.” Food that carries violence does not allow the system to remain peaceful. Start meditation and vegetarian living together — clarity will come.

Beautiful Closing Shares

Several participants spoke with deep emotion. One elder lady (who has followed Yogananda, Babaji, Neem Karoli Baba for years) said that meeting this group felt like the fruit of previous good karma. She felt so much joy that words were not enough. Priyanka and the group responded with the same warmth: “We are all friends. This is our wealth — satsang.”

The session closed with the reminder that we meet again tomorrow at 7 pm with another beautiful topic and meditation.

Expanded Reflection – Why This Teaching Matters

Karma is often the most misunderstood of the six laws. When we treat it as blind punishment, we become victims. When we understand it as precise education, we become students who can graduate.

Three practical shifts arise from today’s session:

  1. Stop asking “Why me?” and start asking “What seed did I sow that is now ripening?” This single change removes resentment and opens responsibility.
  2. Vegetarian living is not a diet rule — it is a direct reduction of new entries into the not-right-karma box. Every meal becomes a choice about the kind of future we are creating.
  3. Meditation is the accelerator. Ordinary living clears karma slowly. Daily Anapanasati clears it at a much higher speed because the soul is no longer creating new reactive karma while sitting in pure awareness.

The two-box picture is especially powerful for daily life. Before any action, word or strong thought, we can quietly ask: “Which box is this going into?” Over time the question itself becomes a protective filter.

Finally, the thorn analogy removes fear of difficulty. Difficulty is not the enemy; unfinished thorns are. When we stop resisting the removal process and cooperate with it through meditation, gratitude and cleaner living, the same life that felt like punishment becomes the classroom of liberation.
