Devi Navaratri · Day 3

Suras & Asuras · Svara · Guṇas · Icchā Śakti · Inner Rhythm

On Day 3, Dr. Newton brings together the whole inner logic of the first three days: who are suras and asuras, what svara and apasvara mean in breath, speech and life, how guṇas shape our tendencies, and why the whole first block of Navaratri is about Icchā-śakti — the power of will and clear desire.

He repeatedly reminds the class that the story is not merely outer mythology. Every asura, every deity, every battlefield is a symbolic map of the human mind. The real war is inside the antaḥkaraṇa, and the real task is alignment.

1. Suras and asuras: the battle is inside

In the Purāṇas, the stories of suras and asuras are not only plots of myth. They are inner psychology in symbolic form. A sura is not merely a divine being in the sky; it is a state of consciousness in which body, breath, mind and ātman are in rhythm.

An asura is the state in which that rhythm is lost: the mind thinks one thing, speaks another, and acts from something else. The body may crave rest, the mind may race for recognition, and the speech may be beyond the right context. This is apasvara.

Dr. Newton stresses that the real asuras are not only in distant stories. They live within us: in our distractions, our greed, our pride, our emotional volatility, our misalignment of thought and action.

2. Svara and apasvara: right note, wrong note

Harmony of body, breath and speech

Svara means the right note, the proper sequence, the right context. In music, a raga remains in svara only when the notes are true. In life, svara means living in the proper rhythm of breath, intention and speech.

Apasvara means the wrong note: saying what should not be said, acting out of context, violating the rhythm of the moment. This is what turns a person into an asura — not because of birth, but because of the quality they carry.

He uses the example of the heart and the breath. The heart has its own rhythm; breath also has a rhythm. When these become irregular, the whole life becomes erratic. The inner “music” is broken. The task is to restore alignment.

3. Three guṇas: not labels but proportions

Everything in creation is woven from three guṇas: sattva, rajas and tamas. The point is not to label a person “good” or “bad,” but to understand the proportion of those forces in life.

  • Tamas keeps the body in inertia and sleep; when it dominates, there is laziness and body-identification.
  • Rajas moves the mind and drives action; when it dominates, there is hunger for recognition, status and force.
  • Sattva brings clarity, serenity and alignment; it is the finest expression of the divine in a sādhaka.

He explains that the task of spiritual life is not to suppress all desire, but to see where desire becomes atyāśā — desire beyond measure. Then the mind is no longer aligned with purpose; it becomes a prison. That is asura patterning.

4. Nirguṇa and the higher state

Newton’s emphasis is that the goal is not merely “being good.” The goal is to cross the three guṇas, to rise beyond the quality-bound field of nature and know the self as nirguṇa.

In the nirguṇa state, the impulse is no longer “I must win, I must be seen, I must take, I must possess.” Instead the impulse is: “Let all be well. Let there be peace. Let me serve. Let my energy be a channel for loka-kalyāṇa.”

This is what he means by the inner form of Divinity: not a moral ego, but a state of self-realisation in which one is not bound by tamas and rajas.

5. Why the first three days matter: Icchā-śakti

Desire is not the enemy; wrong desire is the obstacle

The first three days are associated with Mahādurgā / Kālī / Icchā-śakti. This does not mean “desire is bad.” In spiritual life, no creation is possible without desire. The question is whether desire is alignment or distortion.

When a person’s desire is clear and turned toward Amma, it becomes a force for purification. But when desire is confused, selfish, greedy or driven by ego, it turns into an asura-force.

Newton’s teaching here is practical: write down what you want, what blocks you, and what you are asking the Mother to clear. That is the work of the first three days.

6. Lahiri Mahāśaya and the final desire

He uses the story of Lahiri Mahāśaya to illustrate the importance of a final inner ask. Lahiri was already advanced in ātma-jñāna, but one subtle desire remained. That last attachment could reduce the fullness of realisation.

What does this mean? It means that even advanced seekers can carry residual karmic tendency. The path is not suddenly complete because one has some realisation. The final step requires clarity of desire, surrender and the removal of lingering wanting.

That is why the first three days are so important. The question is not only “What do I want?” but “What do I really want?”

7. The heart of the lesson: what do I really want?

Newton asks the group to deepen the question:

  • What do I want?
  • What do I really really want?
  • If this outer difficulty passed, what in me would be healed?
  • If I lived in peace, what would change?
  • If I knew the self, what would my life become?

Only when the heart answers this honestly can life move out of the urge for outer things and into ātma-jñāna.

8. Fasting in a deeper sense

Day 3 also covers what it means to “fast” spiritually:

  • Mind fasting: release the clutter of thoughts and attention.
  • Mauna: silence of speech and inner restlessness.
  • Reels / social media fasting: reduce the outer pulling of attention.
  • Food fasting: not a rule for all time, but a conscious practice during the nine days.

All of this is aligned with the idea that energy should not scatter outward. During Navaratri, the intention is to gather the energy inward and turn it toward the Divine.

9. Lalitā’s army, Vārāhī, Kālikā and divine forces

Divine forces are not mythology alone

The story of Lalitā’s army, Vārāhī and Kālikā is not just a battle legend. It points to the fact that the power of the Divine is not a single static form; it is a field of energies.

These names are many, but the underlying principle is one: there are inner forces that help the seeker clear blocks. The Mother supports the process of purification. The work is not done by force, but by inner alignment and surrender.

10. The essence of the day

Takeaway: suras live in rhythm and alignment, asuras live in broken rhythm and guṇa distortions. Svara means right note, right context, right breath. The first three days are about gaining clarity of desire (Icchā-śakti), not merely trying to “want less.” The goal is to ask Amma for the right desire, the right direction, and the strength to clear inner blocks.
