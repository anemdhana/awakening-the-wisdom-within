Devi Navaratri · Day 1

Inner Meaning of Navaratri · Dissolving the Ego

Dr. Newton’s approach: he explains the deeper meaning behind every ancient story, and he shows how each character, event, and symbol mirrors a guna within us — guiding us from outer story into inner self-observation.

1. Why these nine days?

These ten days — nine nights of Navaratri and Vijayadaśamī — are not ordinary festival days. They are inner-meaning festivals (antarārtha-sahita). Across Bhārat we celebrate Devi Navaratri; in Telangana the same current flows as Batukamma.

If we do not know the meaning, the festival becomes only ritualistic: dance, some temporary joy, then life returns to normal. Every festival has a great meaning, a paramārtha. Our ancestors recognised and established these methods and traditions especially for sādhakas — those devoted to ātma-jñāna and dhyāna. These days are particularly favourable for them.

2. The Bhāgavata story — why the earth-touch?

Durgā as the infant girl

In the Bhāgavata, for the soul-plan of Kṛṣṇa’s birth as the eighth child and Kaṃsa’s destruction, a design was already complete. According to that plan the children were slain by Kaṃsa; the eighth was exchanged. The girl-child given to Kaṃsa is not ordinary — she is Jaganmātā Durgā, Mahāmāyā, filled with Jaganmāyā-śakti.

Kaṃsa seizes her by the feet to dash her on the stone. The moment her feet receive the touch of the earth, she asks Mahāviṣṇu — her brother — why this earth-touch was given. The answer given in the Purāṇas: in the ages to come, much will unfold on this earth-sphere, and the role of Parameśvarī will be great.

Behind the story is a soul-plan. Parameśvara and Parameśvarī are the root of all creation. She must come; the whole of nature is filled with her. Every festival carries a speciality; behind it a story is told so that we may take it with its inner meaning — dhyāna-parangā, ādhyātmika-parangā.

3. Nimajjanam — creation, sustenance, dissolution

In these festivals we make the mūrti of the Devi and later offer it in water — nimajjanam. Just as we did with Gaṇeśa immersion last month, the same inner design applies here. Whatever is created must also dissolve. We too came into the womb; we too will dissolve. What appears in creation must return into dissolution.

This is not pessimism — it is the structure of reality: sṛṣṭi–sthiti–laya. The outer custom encodes the inner work. When we visit temples and sit in dhyāna, observing the architecture and the śilpas, their inner meaning becomes experiential.

4. The asura who ate himself

Ego dissolves when awareness dawns

In a temple there is a figure of an asura whose quality is to eat whatever he sees — driven by endless hunger. Once, when nothing remained, he began to eat himself: legs finished, arms finished, torso finished, until only the head was left. Then awareness (eruka) arose: if I eat this too, I will not remain; I am dissolving; I will not be.

Spiritual science, jñāna, and the path of dhyāna are exactly this: finishing ourselves as the limited person. We entered creation; we must also complete the dissolution — the true nimajjanam, the withdrawal. As we move toward mokṣa, the limited “I” dissolves.

5. Where does aham live? — Maṇipūra / Hara centre

Aham — “I, I, I am here, my name, my place, this is mine” — lives in the belly. That is why Rāma’s arrows on the head, however many times he cut the heads, did not finish Rāvaṇa. Only when the arrow struck the belly did the destruction happen. The will-centre sits in Maṇipūra, also called the Hara centre.

Spirituality is finishing this limited aham — dissolving the narrow definition of “I”. That definition is small and full of limits. Our truth is the unlimited “I” that fills everywhere. To know that, this hara-work is needed: finishing our separate will, our “I will do this, I will do that.”

That is the real nimajjanam. Behind every festival structure our ṛṣis and yogis placed yoga-secrets and fine knowledge. The customs and traditions carry subtle meanings.

Hara means finishing the limited “I”. Without that, progress in dhyāna is blocked. The limited definition of “I” prevents us from knowing the unlimited Self that fills all.

6. Why “nights”? — Night is for sādhana

Why are they called Navarātri — nine nights — and not nine days? There is an inner meaning. People often take “night” as a symbol of tamas. For yogis it is different. Night is when no one disturbs you — the purest time for sādhana. Rise in the early hours; sit. Day is for work and the world; night is for the inner work.

Devi Navaratri names the nights because these are nights dedicated to the Shakti who is the root of all that is seen as nature and creation. We call her Durgā, Lalitā, Jaganmātā — many names, one Shakti. In Telangana, Batukamma (the festival of “living / life”) carries the same current of celebrating life with its inner meaning.

7. Structure of the nine days

Each day has alankāra, colours, flowers — then on the last day, dissolution. This structure matches sṛṣṭi–sthiti–laya. What is in the macrocosm is in the microcosm; what happens above happens here; what happens in the causal worlds happens in the subtle and the gross.

As above, so below.

8. Nava-āvaraṇas · Śrī Cakra

The nine enclosures (nava-āvaraṇas) of the Śrī Cakra are also named for these nine:

  • Trailokya Mohana — she who enchants the three worlds
  • Sarvāśā Paripūraka — fulfilling all aspirations
  • Sarva Saṃkṣobhaṇa — moving / agitating all
  • Sarva Saubhāgyadāyaka — granting auspiciousness
  • Sarvārthasādhaka — accomplishing all purposes
  • Sarva Rakṣākara — protecting all
  • Sarva Rogahara — removing all disease
  • Sarva Siddhiprada — granting all siddhis
  • Sarvānandamaya — pure ānanda

She is Śrī Cakra-nivāsinī, Śrī Mātā, Śrī Mahārājñī, Śrīmat Siṃhāsaneśvarī — Lalitā, Durgā, Jaganmātā.

9. Three guṇas × three states = nine

Creation is woven of the three guṇas. We move in three states: jāgrat (waking), svapna (dream), suṣupti (deep sleep). Each state can be coloured by tamas, rajas, or sattva — 3 × 3 = 9.

Tamas is the lowest vibration; rajas the middle; sattva the finer. The goal is to cross the three guṇas. Beyond them is the nirguṇa state — our true ātma-svarūpa, the form of Parabrahman, of Parameśvarī and Parameśvara. In the world everything is with guṇa; our essence is without.

Navaratri is the laboratory to move from guṇa-bound living toward the nirguṇa centre.

10. How to hold these nine days

  • Do not treat the festival as entertainment only — hold the inner meaning.
  • Increase sādhana; night and early morning (brāhmī muhūrta) are especially favourable.
  • Watch the limited “I” (aham) — name, story, ownership — and practice dissolving it in awareness.
  • See difficult people (even family) as indirect teachers when old karma ripens.
  • Keep the feeling: everything is Amma / Shakti; first bond with her.

11. Questions & Answers from the session

At the end of the session participants shared and asked. Gratitude came first — for daily classes, for the way the teaching moves in the body, for the motivation it gives when serving society. Then a deeper question was taken.

Participant (Lakṣmī Kalyāṇi)

In previous births, what was the bond with husband and children? In this life my own children have become like enemies. Why? I already understand cause and effect — “I did this, so it is returning.” When they insult me I can see: this is what I once did. Even so, the pain continues for months. Why does the pain remain even after understanding? Please explain a little.

Dr Newton

The answer is already inside your question. You said: “In some past birth, because of karma, my children have become enemies.” That is true. On this earth-sphere there is only cause and effect — no event without a cause.

You are already attending this programme, receiving knowledge, doing dhyāna-sādhana. Knowledge related to what you are living is given; experiences come. But one must wait. Keep practising and keep listening. Sometimes jñāna-vīcikas rise from within — an inner wave of knowing — and the viewpoint shifts further. You already reported a shift: “They are giving back what I once gave.”

Now go one step more. Hold them as indirect gurus — placed in your own house to deliver lessons. Through them you are meant to bring forth kṣamā (forgiveness) and karuṇā (compassion). Even when they speak harshly, love them unconditionally. That is also a lesson: unconditional love — the love that should have been given to children without conditions when they were small. What they did not receive then, they project back when they grow up: “You were not there; you did not give time; you scolded.”

All of this can clear. Stay in this sādhana these nine days. Do a little more each day — even one extra hour of dhyāna raises energy levels and the angle of seeing changes. See them as teachers who took birth in your house to give you certain lessons. When the lessons are received, the enemy-feeling softens; they will call you “Amma” again. Patience, endurance and continuous practice are needed. Do not leave sādhana even when it is hard. They behave firmly so that the lessons land. When the lessons land, everything clears.

Cause and effect is the law. Understanding stops the story of “why me”; sādhana dissolves the remaining pain. Difficult people in the house can be the most precise teachers.

Homework

  • Morning dhyāna as in Jñānodayam — Anāpāna-sati; stay with the natural breath. Sit steady in sukhāsana; if thoughts come, gently return attention to the breath.
  • Listen to / recite Mahishāsura Mardinī stotra (Aigiri Nandini…). Read it two or three times; sit with its meaning in meditation. Tomorrow the story will be decoded further.
  • Through the day: notice aham — “I, mine, my story” — and gently release.
  • Extra sādhana if family difficulty is intense: even one hour more of practice raises energy and shifts the angle of seeing.

Remember

  • Festivals without inner meaning become empty ritual.
  • Nimajjanam = dissolution of the limited self.
  • Aham lives in the belly (Maṇipūra / Hara) — that is what must finish.
  • Night is sādhana-time for the yogi.
  • Nine days · nine āvaraṇas · three guṇas × three states.
  • Beyond the three guṇas is nirguṇa — our true nature.
  • Difficult people can be indirect teachers; respond with sādhana, not only reaction.
