Devi Navaratri · Day 2

Shakti & Chaitanya · Devi Samarpan Sādhana

On Day 2, Dr. Newton returns to the core of the entire Navaratri teaching: Shakti and Chaitanya. This is not a ritual day only. It is the day of seeing that the whole universe is a manifestation of divine power and divine awareness, and that the seeker must turn inward to bring that power into living practice.

He gives a very clear orientation: there are only two essential points to hold in the mind — Shakti and Chaitanya. Everything else becomes secondary. If one knows these two deeply, one begins to live in the inner meaning of the festival, not just the outer form.

1. Why Navaratri is not mere enjoyment

Dr. Newton distinguishes sharply between enjoyment and celebration.

  • Enjoyment is driven by desire. “I want this, I enjoy that.” It remains on the level of pleasure, craving, and sensory motion.
  • Celebration or ānanda is deeper. One does not live from “what do I want?” but from a state of inner illumination and gratitude.

Devi Navaratri is not simply a festive mood or a social occasion. It is a period especially favourable for Shakti sādhana. Those who are struggling, those who feel “answers have not yet come,” are told that if they practise sincerely, the result is real. The availability of Parāśakti and Ādiśakti is said to be especially potent at this time.

2. Only two points to know — Shakti and Chaitanya

In the spiritual teaching, the essential points are really only two:

  • Shakti — Parvatī Mātā, Durgā, Ādiśakti, Parāśakti, Parameśvarī. Many names, one power.
  • Chaitanya — Īśvara, God, Sarveśvara, Sarvāntaryāmī, the all-pervading consciousness.

If we do not know Shakti, we do not truly know Chaitanya. In Bhārata, the nine nights are given to lead the seeker toward this inner recognition. It is a sacred time for knowing, feeling, and living the Divine as power and awareness.

The entire universe is also Shakti and Chaitanya. These two alone are the points to hold.

3. The direction of energy: inward vs outward

He explains that Shakti has two primary directions:

  • Energy that comes inward — the feminine principle, internal, aligned with the heart and ātman. This is associated with the left nostril and candra-nāḍī.
  • Energy that goes outward — the masculine/external direction, action, movement, thinking, outward engagement. This corresponds to the right side, outward expression.

To know this is crucial. The goal during these nine days is not to scatter energy outward, not to waste it in distraction, complaint, and external chasing. The task is to turn the power inward, preserve it, and multiply it within.

That is the spiritual logic of the festival: not “I am doing; I am being busy,” but “I am gathering my energy into the core of my being.”

4. Lakṣmī mudrā and the sitting posture

The posture acts as a symbol

He teaches that in these days one sits in a way that is favourable to inward gathering. The Lakṣmī mudrā is the posture of sitting with the left leg over the right leg, left hand over the right hand, or otherwise preserving the feminine-inward orientation.

The message is not cosmetic. It is symbolic: the seeker is learning to hold energy, not scatter it. Without Shakti, even Śiva remains like a corpse, a potential without movement. Shakti gives motion, transformation, and life.

5. The three forms of the Divine Mother

Parāśakti is worshipped in many forms, but mainly in three primary expressions:

  • Sarasvatī Mātā — knowledge, vidyā, jñāna
  • Lakṣmī Mātā — prosperity, aiśvarya, abundance
  • Durgā / Kālī Mātā — power that removes obstacles and destroys what obstructs practice

These are not separated powers; they are different expressions of one divine force. Dr. Newton also gives the three seed syllables associated with them:

  • Aiṃ — Sarasvatī
  • Śrīm — Lakṣmī
  • Klīm — Kālī / Durgā

These sound vibrations are understood as carriers of divine energy. In this mode, the seeker begins to feel: “I am not separate from Amma; I am in her field.”

6. Bowing the head — the reduction of ego

Another central point is bowing. In Indian culture, even from childhood, we are taught to bow to elders, to the teacher, to the divine. The head must bend. This is not merely external tradition; it is inner psychology.

When the head is bent, the ego reduces. When the ego is reduced, the mind becomes receptive. That is why in the temple, as one moves from outer hall to inner sanctum, the doorway becomes smaller and smaller. The body learns humility. The mind learns not to stand in a posture of self-importance.

During these nine days, this is the practice: head down, ego soft, offering to Amma.

7. Total acceptance

The heart must stop resisting

Dr. Newton emphasises one of the most practical teachings of the day: acceptance.

Accept the past completely. Do not keep replaying “if only I had done it differently.” Accept the present as it is. Accept the people around you as they are. Do not reject, do not deny, do not build a narrative of complaint around your life.

He gives a simple analogy: even a bitter gourd may not be liked, but one can still accept it without making it into a permanent grievance. Similarly, one must accept the reality of the present and move inward rather than scatter energy toward complaint.

Non-acceptance is a form of agitation. Acceptance is a form of inner gathering.

8. Mother first — then Father

He makes a powerful point: in these nine days, the order of relationship matters.

“First Amma,” not “first Father.” We may love Father, but the primary connection the seeker must cultivate is with the Mother — with Shakti, with the feminine source of power. The seeker climbs through Jaganmātā and then reaches the higher understanding of Śiva/Chaitanya.

The deeper insight is that all forms eventually lead to the same reality, yet the path begins in Shakti. Without the mother-energy, there is no movement inward. Without Shakti, the divine is inert.

9. The central practice: Devi Samarpan

Dr. Newton then gives the central practice of the day: Devi Samarpan.

This is a simple but powerful sequence. It can be done in the morning and again in the evening. The attitude is not mechanical; it is a sincere act of surrender.

  1. Viṣṇu mudrā — close the index and middle fingers, keep the others open. Gently close the right nostril with the right hand. Do not press hard.
  2. Inhale through the left nostril nine times. This is the inward current, the candra-nāḍī, the feminine side of the breath.
  3. Bow the head in namaskāra or simply with a feeling of total offering. “I offer myself to this Shakti — Jaganmātā, Parāśakti, the Shakti within me.”
  4. Hold the breath and silently repeat Śrīm nine times in the mind. No need to chant loudly. The key is the inner vibration.
  5. Exhale and surrender. As the breath leaves, offer all worries, problems, attachments, and desires to the Mother. “Śaraṇu, śaraṇu, śaraṇu.”

Repeat the full cycle for as many rounds as possible, ideally 9 rounds. The number nine is significant: it carries the feeling of completion, unfolding, and cosmic rhythm. But the spiritual value is not the count itself; it is the state of surrender that the count helps produce.

When one truly surrenders, one begins to feel a difference: the burden is no longer held as “mine alone.” The energy is no longer a private possession. It is offered and multiplied.

10. The sense of red and the dynamic force

Dr. Newton also connects the red colour of the festival to the idea of dynamic Shakti. Red in dress, red in food, red in the symbolic atmosphere of the season is not arbitrary. It marks the expression of dynamic energy — the active, living force of Devi.

The point is not to become externally symbolic alone; it is to orient the mind and body toward the inner source of power and vitality.

11. The deeper meaning of the nine days

These nine days are not simply a calendar event. They are a field of inward repositioning. Their purpose is to help the seeker:

  • turn energy inward
  • accept and soften the ego
  • offer the breath, body, mind and emotions to the Divine Mother
  • live not in complaint but in surrender

He explains that by Vijayadaśamī — the tenth day — a sincere practice can produce a genuine inward transformation. It is not an outer celebration only. It is the celebration of the self being reoriented toward the Divine source.

Homework

  • Do the Devi Samarpan practice in the morning, ideally 1 round, or 9 if possible.
  • Repeat once in the evening if you can.
  • Throughout the day, practise total acceptance instead of complaint.
  • Keep the mind in the awareness that the whole universe is Shakti and Chaitanya.
  • Bow inwardly to Amma, and offer the body, breath, and mind to her.

Remember

Only two points matter: Shakti and Chaitanya. Navaratri is not enjoyment alone; it is a powerful period for inward transformation. Energy must be turned inward, not scattered outward. The posture, the breath, the bow, the offering, and the total acceptance are all part of one inner process. Devi Samarpan is the practical expression of surrender: inhale from the left nostril, bow, repeat Śrīm in the mind, release the breath, and offer everything to the Mother. This is how the inner field is purified.
